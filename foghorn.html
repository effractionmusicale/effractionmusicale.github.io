Modelling a foghorn in Faust, by measurement

An AI's working session, told in its own words — Claude Code + faustcode, IEEE IS2 2026

This is the log of one working session in which an AI (Claude, driving the faustcode web IDE through its MCP tools) modelled a recorded ship’s foghorn in Faust. It worked deaf. Each version was compiled and rendered offline by faustcode, measured in Python against the recording, then played live in the browser. A human (Yann Orlarey) listened and said what was still wrong. The session went through 40 versions. This document follows the 8 where something important happened.

Each milestone below has its render, to listen to, and a button that opens the Faust code in faustcode, where you can play it, change it and look at its block diagram. All 40 versions are listed in the appendix. v1–v11 are single blasts; from v12 on, the renders contain the target’s full three-blast sequence (a pattern button in the model).

The prompt

The whole session started from this request, pasted as is:

The target recording is target/foghorn.wav. Model this foghorn in Faust using the faustcode MCP.

Do not fall into the trap of static additive synthesis — instead, faithfully reproduce the full spectral evolution, especially during the attack that gives a ship’s foghorn its brassy, broad, and very present sound.

Work by measurement: first extract the target’s characteristics (envelope by phase, f₀ with sub-harmonic correction, harmonic profile, HNR, roughness), then iterate by comparing your rendering to the target on these same metrics at every step. Decide on the synthesis approach based on what the measurements say, and let me know when you need my ear.

Save each version as work/horn_vN.dsp and submit it to faustcode so that we can all listen to it.

A short notes file in the working folder (CLAUDE.md) added practical details: which faustcode tools render and play a version, how to script the gate button in an offline render, and which Python to use for the analysis. Everything else came from the measurements and from the listener’s remarks.

The target, and the approach it suggested

Target

target/foghorn.wav is a stereo recording, 22.6 s at 48 kHz, of three blasts: 3.36 s, 2.86 s and 1.59 s long. The first analysis (analyze.py … analyze4.py) found:

  • Pitch: f0 = 130.8 Hz, with H2 the strongest harmonic. The sub-harmonic correction matters here, since a naive f0 estimator would report 261 Hz. The pitch is very stable during the blast (σ ≈ 0.1 Hz), but each blast starts with a glide from about 113 Hz up to 131 Hz.
  • Attack: about 150–200 ms. The spectrum opens with the amplitude. At onset H1 is alone (H2 at −24 dB), and by +150 ms all harmonics are present. That is the brassy part, and a static harmonic recipe cannot produce it.
  • Harmonic profile in sustain: H1 at −5 dB, H2 at 0, then a broad decline with notches near H6, H11, H17 and H22. The HNR is 26.6 dB.
  • Slow darkening: the spectral centroid falls from about 390 to 310 Hz over a blast.
  • Cut-off: identical on every blast. In the last ~50 ms the spectrum darkens, the level bumps up ~2.5 dB and the pitch dips 2 %. That looks like a valve slamming shut.
  • Tail: the level holds at about −9 dB for ~0.7 s after the cut, then decays at about −20 dB/s.
  • Stereo: two spaced microphones. The right channel is 4.67 ms late and correlated at 0.78 at that lag.

Approach. These measurements describe a driven valve rather than a set of partials. So the model is a valve source whose blowing pressure controls three things at once:

  • the valve opening, which sets the pulse shape and hence the brightness;
  • the pitch glide;
  • the breath noise.

After the source come a horn body (a reflection and a few resonances) and a space (microphones, echoes, reverb). The harmonics are never specified one by one: they come out of a single pulse whose shape changes with pressure.

All comparisons used small Python scripts (numpy/scipy) written during the session, aligned on the blast onsets. faustcode’s built-in metrics (audio_metrics_v2) assume a single attack–sustain–release note: here the valve slam at the cut is louder than the attack overshoot, so they reported a 67 ms “sustain”. Custom scripts on faustcode’s renders took over, and this case is now feedback for the tool.

Milestone v2: a smooth valve pulse instead of a clipped cosine

v1Open v1 in faustcode
v2Open v2 in faustcode

Measured. v1 modelled the valve flow as a clipped cosine, max(0, cos(θ) − c)^q, with c following the pressure. The render lost its odd harmonics: H5, H7, H9 and H11 came out between −30 and −50 dB, against −13 to −37 dB in the target. It was also 14 dB too quiet and too clean (HNR 35.6 against 26.6 dB).

Hypothesis. The clipped cosine has sinc-like nulls whose positions move with c. Near c ≈ 0 it becomes a half-wave rectified cosine, which has almost no odd harmonics. A simulation of the chain (sim_source.py) confirmed it: at c = −0.1, H5 falls to −44 dB.

Changed. The valve became a pulse with a smooth, geometric spectrum, Σ rᵏ cos kθ, in closed form. Here r → 0 gives a sine and r → 1 a sharp pulse, so r becomes the brightness control. The body was fitted on the target’s 20 sustain harmonics (1.6 dB rms): an inverted reflection plus two resonances.

pulse = (r * cos(th) - r * r) / (1 - 2 * r * cos(th) + r * r) / r;
comb(x) = x - 0.975 * de.fdelay(256, 0.001331 * ma.SR, x);
body = comb : fi.peak_eq(7.4, 257, 43) : fi.peak_eq(13.2, 805, 211);

Next measurement. The dry render’s harmonics landed within about 2–4 dB of the target. A Python simulation of the attack also revealed a bug: a “reservoir sag” term was holding the sustain brightness at r ≈ 0.94 instead of the fitted 0.97. That was fixed in v3.

Milestone v8: the harbour is a far wall, not a flutter echo

v8Open v8 in faustcode

Measured. v4–v7 placed the recording’s spaced microphones, a flutter echo and a reverb around the source. The sustain spectrum filled with deep notches (H1 at −24 dB, H4 at −36 dB) that the source did not have: a dry render (render_v6_dry.wav) was clean. In v7 the reverb dominated and the level crept up by 7 dB over the blast.

Hypothesis. Looking again at the tail: it stays flat for 0.7 s after the cut and only then decays. Flat means the reflected energy arrives about 0.7 s late, from a reflector roughly 120 m away (the far side of a harbour). The same reflection explains a spectral change in the target about 0.63 s after every onset.

Changed. An echo cluster at 0.62–0.74 s, with its path “wandering in the wind”, feeds a reverb kept about 12 dB below the direct sound. The space is just one block, routed with Faust’s split (<:) and merge (:>):

echoes = _ <: cluster(0.62), cluster(0.64);
space  = _ <: (_ <: _, _), (echoes <: (_, _), (rev : *(wet), *(wet))) :> _, _;

Next measurement. The harmonics came back (H1–H10 within about 2–4 dB), the centroid read 327 Hz against 334, and the tail plateau was right up to about 1.3 s. A surprise: with less harmonic energy from the space, the noise was now 8 dB too strong (HNR 18.2 dB). v9 fixed that.

Milestone v13: a horn that only sings near full pressure

v13Open v13 in faustcode

Measured. In v12 the brightness timing matched the target, but the level rose too slowly: −5.1 dB at +0.1 s, where the target is already at −0.3 dB. The pressure overshoot also arrived about 0.5 s late.

Hypothesis. Rather than guess again, the exact Faust envelope formulas were re-implemented in Python (fit_buildup.py), including the far echo, and fitted to 8 onset windows of the target. The fit wanted amplitude = pressure^23. In other words, the valve is almost silent until the pressure is nearly full, then bursts into oscillation. That is plausible for a diaphragm horn.

Changed.

pr   = gate : ar(0.077, 1.15) : ar(0.077, 1.15);
amp0 = pr^23;
f0   = f0n * (1 - 0.14 * (1 - amp0)^4) * ...;   // the pitch glide now follows the oscillation

Next measurement. On the full 22.6 s file the RMS envelope matched within 3.1 dB (correlation 0.987), and all three onsets landed within 20 ms of the target’s. The rise was still 2–3 dB short at 0.1–0.2 s; v14 fixed it by letting the H2 resonance build up earlier. A second echo at about 1.5 s appeared in the target’s tails and was added.

Milestone v20: the ear hears steps in the decay

v19Open v19 in faustcode
v20Open v20 in faustcode

Heard. On v19: “the reverb part doesn’t sound natural, we can hear clear steps during the decay.” None of the existing metrics captured this. A new one was written (steps.py): the worst 60 ms drop in the first 2 s of each tail. It came out at 3.2–3.7 dB for the target and 8.2–9.7 dB for v19.

Hypothesis. Each echo was a clean copy of the blast, so when it ended the listener heard a second, delayed cut. And the reverb was fed only by the echoes, so it did not start until they arrived.

Changed. v20 went back to v14’s source (see Backtracking). Each echo now passes through its own allpass diffuser, and a little diffused direct sound feeds the reverb.

diffL = ap(0.011, 0.7) : ap(0.017, 0.7) : ap(0.029, 0.65) : ap(0.043, 0.65) : ap(0.061, 0.6);
echoL = _ <: wander(0.66, 0.0025, 0.17, _) * 0.30, wander(1.55, 0.0035, 0.13, _) * 0.07 :> diffL;

Next measurement. The steps were roughly halved (worst drop 4.0, 5.4 and 5.2 dB on the three tails). The tails matched within 3.2 dB rms with no bias, and the full envelope correlation reached 0.991. The ear was not satisfied for long, though (next milestone).

Milestone v24 → v30: the harbour rebuilt with library reverbs, by ear

v27Open v27 in faustcode
v30Open v30 in faustcode

Heard. “Why not use an existing reverb in the Faust library?” That was a better question than it first looked, and the next seven versions followed it.

  • v22–v23, re.greyhole (a diffuser in feedback with a long modulated delay). It washed on instead of decaying (about −2 dB/s), its feedback coloured the blast itself (harmonic error ~7 dB), and it took more than 60 s to compile in the browser. Abandoned.
  • v24, re.zita_rev1_stereo fed directly by the horn. The verdict was “much better” (bien mieux, in French). By the numbers its reverb was 13 dB too weak, but the ear preferred its smoothness.
  • v25, zita behind a 0.55 s pre-delay, to recreate the plateau. When zita’s input stopped, its output dropped 7 dB within 50 ms: a new cliff. In this setting, most of zita’s steady-state energy is early, first-pass energy.
  • v26–v27, re.stereo_freeverb behind the pre-delay, calibrated with silent parameter sweeps. The tail matched within 1.9 dB, the best of all versions. The ear then removed the pre-delay (v28), and the middle ground at 0.2 s (v29) measured about the same as none.
  • Heard on v29: “the release still has a kind of amplitude modulation which is not in the original.” A new metric (tail_am.py, the modulation spectrum of the detrended tail envelope) found 6.2–6.8 Hz in freeverb’s tail, against ~1 Hz in the target. On a steady harmonic tone, freeverb’s comb resonances near each harmonic beat against each other after the cut. zita’s denser network fluctuated at 1–2 Hz, like the target.

Changed (v30). zita fed directly, with T60 set from the target’s decay slope:

rev   = re.zita_rev1_stereo(rdel, 200, 2500, t60l, t60m, 48000);
space = _ <: (_ <: _, _), (_ <: rev : *(wet), *(wet)) :> _, _;

Next measurement and ear. Release modulation at 2.0 / 1.0 Hz (L/R) and a slope of −15.4 dB/s, against −15.9. “It sounds much more natural.” The tail sits about 6 dB under the target in its first second: that is the plateau the ear chose to give up.

Milestone v31: more wind, less metal

v31Open v31 in faustcode

Heard. “The original has more wind and it is less metallic.”

Measured (timbre.py, on a steady 1.6 s window of blast 2): - the noise between harmonics was 7 dB short below 200 Hz; - above 8 kHz the target is noise-dominated (harmonic/noise ratio −4.1 dB), while v30 was tonal (+9.5 dB); - a cepstrum of the spectrum showed comb peaks about 7 dB stronger than the target’s, plus an extra one at 2.66 ms from the dispersion stage.

Hypothesis. Two problems. The breath noise went through the horn body, so it was comb-filtered, which sounds metallic. And the dispersion used allpass combs, short delay lines that ring.

Changed. The noise became jet noise added after the body, shaped like the target’s noise floor. The reflection gain went from 1.0 to 0.75, the allpass combs were replaced by first-order allpasses with no delay line, and a 6 kHz rolloff was added on the tone.

turb = no.noise * ra^3 * (1 + pulse * 0.1) * wind : fi.highpass(1, 40) : fi.lowpass(1, 500) : fi.lowpass(2, 6500);
ap1(a) = \(x).(x' - a * x : + ~ *(a));                 // first-order allpass
horn = (flow * 20 : body) + turb : *(vol);

Next measurement. The noise now matched within ~2.5 dB in every band, and the harmonic/noise ratio above 8 kHz was −3.6 dB. A surprise: the comb peaks fell to +8.6 dB, below the target’s +15.7. v32 brought the reflection gain back to 0.9, together with a broader body resonance (718 Hz, 347 Hz wide), after the ear asked for a “fatter” sound.

Milestone v33: the blowing starts with the tone

v33Open v33 in faustcode

Heard. “Still a little bit different, in particular during the attack: there is more blowing in the original.”

Measured (attack_noise.py, 40 ms frames around each onset). At onset, the target’s 2–8 kHz band is already at −10 dB relative to its sustain, and noise-like (flatness 0.24). In v32 the same band was at −64 dB, and it only filled in when the harmonics arrived at +150 ms.

Hypothesis. The noise followed the oscillation (amp³), while physically the jet noise follows the airflow. That flow starts with the tone, with a burst while the pressure overshoots.

Changed.

blow = min(1, 8 * amp0) * shut * (1 + 1.5 * bump);
turb = no.noise * blow * (1 + pulse * 0.1) * wind : ...;

Next measurement and ear. The 2–8 kHz band read −9.2, −6.9, −6.7 and −5.6 dB at 0, 40, 80 and 120 ms, against −10.3, −9.9, −5.6 and +0.2 dB in the target. “The blowing now is quite similar.” One measured difference remains: the brass opens about 40 ms later than in the target.

Milestone v36 → v40: fat and powerful were about stereo and loudness

v36Open v36 in faustcode
v40Open v40 in faustcode

Heard. First “not as fat”, then “the original seems more powerful, from a bigger horn”.

Measured. v34 already matched the target’s mono spectrum within 1.5 dB per third-octave band, so the remaining difference had to be elsewhere:

  1. Stereo (stereo.py). The target’s channels are decorrelated at zero lag (−0.03) and offset by 4.65 ms. Mine were mostly mono (+0.79): since v5 the direct sound had been common to both channels, to avoid comb filtering in my mono-sum metrics. Measured per channel (perchan.py), the target has H1 as strong as H2. Its mono sum hides about 4 dB of H1 and H4 because of the offset between the mics, so I had been fitting the wrong spectrum.
  2. The reverb was eating the fundamental. After adding the spaced pair (v35), per-channel H1 got worse (−8 dB relative to H2). The steady H1 from the reverb was cancelling the direct one. The target’s tail has H1 14–15 dB under H2, so v36 highpasses the reverb send.
  3. Loudness and density (loudness.py). v36 played about 6.5 dB quieter than the target (−13.0 against −6.5 LUFS), with a much peakier waveform (crest 10.6 against 7.7 dB). A soft saturation after the body barely moved the crest (v37), and placing it before the body (v38) did nothing at all: the body re-shapes the waveform. An offline test (crest_test.py) showed that four second-order allpasses around the low harmonics bring the crest to 8.9 dB without changing the spectrum. A gentle saturation then brings it to 8.0 dB.

Changed.

mics  = _ <: *(0.87), (de.delay(1024, int(0.00465 * ma.SR)) : *(1.15));
space = _ <: mics, (fi.highpass(4, 230) <: rev : *(wet), *(wet)) :> _, _;
ap2(fc, r) = fi.tf2(r * r, c, 1, c, r * r) with { c = -2 * r * cos(2 * ma.PI * fc / ma.SR); };
throat(x) = room * ma.tanh(x / room);

Next measurement and ear.

Backtracking

The worst detour: v14 → v15–v19 → v20. v14’s tone faded slowly and darkly for ~0.5 s after the cut. I took that for a release bug and “fixed” it in v15. The following versions then tried single echoes, later bounces, a weaker reverb and a shorter one. A table over the full file showed every one of them doing worse than v14 on the tails (rms error 4.4 dB for v14, 7.0 dB for v19): the target’s frames after the cut look just like v14’s slow dark fade. v20 restarted from v14’s source, and v21 gave the fade its measured shape (τ = 0.2 s). The lesson: compare against the whole recording regularly, not only against the last version.

Smaller reversals followed the same pattern: a change, a measurement, and a return when it made things worse (narrow EQ cuts in v11, re.greyhole in v22–v23, the pre-delay in v25–v29, saturation before the body in v38). Measurement also corrected a few wrong guesses: a reflection read as 1.39 ms was fitted at 1.336 ms, and an apparent 0.3 % pitch offset was an artefact of short analysis windows.

Why Faust helps here

In Faust, the code is the block diagram, and every block has a precise mathematical meaning: a function from input signals to output signals. That is what let an AI reason about the horn piece by piece, and trace a defect to one block (“it’s the space, not the source”) instead of to the program as a whole. What this session actually relied on:

  • The model is a composition of named blocks. The whole horn is about a hundred lines. Faust’s composition operators make the signal flow the code itself: : (in sequence), , (in parallel), <: (split), :> (merge), ~ (feedback). For example, the final body is one line: comb : lowmode : fi.peak_eq(8.7, 718, 347) : residual : eq : disperse : fi.lowpass(2, 4500).
  • One block can change without touching the others. The reverb changed five times (hand-built echoes, greyhole, zita, freeverb, zita again), and each time only space changed. The dispersion stage was swapped several times (allpass combs, first-order allpasses, then added second-order sections) without touching the body around it.
  • Small, readable diffs. Most versions were derived from the previous one by a handful of exact, checked replacements, typically 5–15 changed lines. That keeps every step reviewable, and is what makes this document possible.
  • The equations are explicit, so they can be fitted. Envelopes like ar(0.077, 1.15) and pr^23 are a few lines of maths. They were re-implemented in Python, fitted to the target, and the fitted numbers went straight back into the Faust code (v13).
  • A library of tested blocks. re.zita_rev1_stereo, re.stereo_freeverb, re.greyhole, fi.peak_eq, fi.lowshelf, fi.tf2, de.fdelay and no.lfnoise were all used directly.
  • The user interface is part of the code. Every hslider or checkbox declared in the model is a parameter that can be set from outside. Wet level, T60, pre-delay or saturation could thus be swept as experiments, and a dry checkbox isolated the source from the space; that is how the notches (v6) and the recurring steps in the tail (v27) were traced to the space, not the source.
  • The compiler. All 40 versions compiled without errors, so this session gives no example of the compiler catching a mistake. The one practical cost was compile time: the two greyhole versions (v22, v23) took more than a minute to compile in the browser.

What faustcode brought

faustcode is a Faust IDE that runs entirely in the browser: the Faust compiler itself runs there, compiled to WebAssembly. Through its MCP server, the AI drove the same IDE the audience was watching. Over the session it made about 50 submissions, 90 offline renders and 65 live triggers:

  • Compile and run, with nothing to install. Each submit compiled the new version in the browser and showed its code, block diagram and controls in the browser tab.
  • Offline renders as a measuring instrument. render_audio rendered a version with chosen parameter values and scripted button presses (the gate, then the pattern of three blasts), and wrote a WAV file that Python could analyse. The parameter sweeps of v24–v29 and v37–v40 were silent renders of this kind.
  • The same code, live in the room. Right after each measurement, run_audio and trigger_button played the version through the browser, so the human ear judged exactly what had been measured.
  • The Faust libraries at hand. The AI looked up function signatures (zita_rev1_stereo, tf2…) with faustcode’s library search instead of guessing them.
  • Shareable versions. Every version is a self-contained link: the buttons on this page open it in faustcode, on a laptop or a phone, with its sliders ready to play.

What the human ear added

Each of these remarks pointed at something no existing metric was looking at. Each one became a new, targeted measurement that then guided the fix:

Heard What the metrics had missed New measurement
“clear steps during the decay” (v19) my tail metric averaged them away worst 60 ms drop (steps.py)
the pre-delay sounded less natural (v27) the metric preferred it (1.9 dB tail error) none: the ear decided
“a kind of amplitude modulation” in the release (v29) no metric looked at modulation in the tail modulation spectrum (tail_am.py)
“more wind, less metallic” (v30) averages hid the noise colour and the comb peaks noise per band and cepstrum (timbre.py)
“more blowing during the attack” (v32) my sustain metrics never looked at the first 100 ms 2–8 kHz band and flatness at onset (attack_noise.py)
“not as fat”, “more powerful, from a bigger horn” (v34–v36) my spectra were mono sums, normalised in level stereo, per-channel spectrum and loudness (stereo.py, perchan.py, loudness.py)

The ear also noticed that a live f0 slider had been left at 102 Hz, which none of my renders checked (they forced 130.8 Hz).

The lesson is not that measurement was wasted. It found causes the ear could not name: half-wave rectification, comb beating, cancellation between the reverb and the direct sound. But what to measure kept coming from listening. The metrics described the sound as averages on a mono sum at normalised level, while the ear heard it in stereo, at real level, moment by moment.

Play it yourself

The final model, adapted for phones: scan the code or open the link, wait for faustcode to compile it, then hold gate to blow and tilt the phone left or right to change the pitch (110–155 Hz). With a whole room playing, everyone’s horn is slightly out of tune with the others, as in a real harbour.
